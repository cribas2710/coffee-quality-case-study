# Phase 6 — Act: Recommendations

**Audience:** General Manager and Board of Directors (decision), agronomists and mill supervisors (execution).
**Evidence base:** [Analysis summary](04_analysis_summary.md) · [Dashboard](https://public.tableau.com/views/Whatmakesgreatcoffee-Honduras/HondurasQualityDashboard) · [Notebook](../notebooks/coffee_quality_analysis.ipynb)

> All findings are **associations** among samples submitted to CQI (2010–2018). They show where the evidence points, not guaranteed results; each recommendation includes a way to measure whether it works for the cooperative.

## Answers to the Success Criteria

**"Where do we stand against our competitors?"**
Honduras ranks **18th of 19** producing countries (mean 80.88 vs. 82.15 overall), significantly below Colombia, Costa Rica and Brazil. The gap has two parts:

| Problem | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Consistency:** occasional lots with defective cups | 5 of 52 samples, all from 2014, **none** reached specialty grade (69–76 points) | Not seen in 2015–2017, but nothing guarantees it will not come back |
| **Ceiling:** rarely excellent coffee | Even without defective cups, Honduras trails on all 7 sensory attributes; excellent rate (≥ 85) **2.1% vs. 10.4%** for competitors | Still open |

**"Which 2–3 investments are most likely to raise our specialty rate?"**
The three below, in order. Altitude and changing the processing method are **not** among them: altitude is a marker of high-quality origins rather than a lever (its association with score nearly disappears within each country), and processing methods differ by less than one point.

## Top 3 Recommendations

### 1. Protect consistency with a lot-level quality control system

**Why.** A defective cup is associated with the largest score loss in the data (**−3.77 points**; specialty rate 45.6% vs. 93.7%). In Honduras, every sample with a defective cup missed specialty grade: without them, Honduras' specialty rate would be **93.6% instead of 84.6%**. The problem disappeared after 2014, which shows it is solvable, but there is no evidence that a system keeps it from coming back.

**Actions**
- **Cup every lot before export** (5 cups per lot, as CQI does) and hold back lots where any cup fails.
- Written protocols for the steps where cup defects typically originate: **fermentation time, drying (moisture 10–12%) and storage**.
- Record every rejection with its cause, to find which step or farm fails most often.

**Measure:** 0 exported lots with a defective cup; specialty rate ≥ 93% (Honduras' rate without defective cups).
**Cost and speed:** low cost, mostly training and procedures; results in the **first harvest**.

### 2. Raise the ceiling with a cup-quality program

**Why.** Defects explain the low tail, not the ceiling. Honduran samples with clean cups still score below competitors on **every** attribute, most on **balance (−0.24) and aftertaste (−0.20)**. Because the attributes move together (ρ = 0.56–0.81), this points to overall cup quality rather than one isolated flaw. Only **1 of 52** Honduran samples reached 85+.

**Actions**
- **Selective picking** of ripe cherries and sorting before processing (industry practices associated with balance and aftertaste; not measured in this data).
- **Controlled fermentation** with recorded times and temperatures, so good results can be repeated.
- Start with lots **above 1,400 m** as specialty candidates. Altitude is used here as a **selection criterion**, not as a lever: higher bands score better worldwide, and Honduras has no samples above 1,650 m.
- Train or hire a cupper to score lots on the same 7 attributes, so progress can be tracked.

**Measure:** share of lots ≥ 85 points (from 1.9% toward the competitors' 10%); mean balance and aftertaste scores per harvest.
**Cost and speed:** medium cost (training, sorting, cupping capacity); results in **2–3 harvests**.

### 3. Test new processing methods with a pilot before switching

**Why.** Processing methods differ by less than one point. Within the same country, washed and natural score alike (not significant in 8 of 9 countries, including Honduras). Semi-washed and honey look slightly better, but the first is mostly a Brazil and Mexico effect and the second has only 14 samples.

**Actions**
- Process a **few lots** with honey or semi-washed alongside the usual washed process, with the same cherries.
- Cup both versions with the same protocol and compare.
- Switch only if the pilot shows a consistent gain over at least two harvests.

**Measure:** score difference between pilot and control lots.
**Cost and speed:** low cost and low risk; it avoids a large investment that the evidence does not support.

### Foundation: measure quality at lot level

None of the three recommendations can be evaluated without the cooperative's **own data**. CQI samples are the best lots producers choose to submit, and they cannot show which farm practices move the score. A simple lot record (farm, altitude, harvest date, process, fermentation and drying times, moisture, defects, cupping scores) turns every harvest into evidence.

## What We Do Not Recommend

| Option | Why not |
|---|---|
| Invest in higher-altitude land | The association with score nearly disappears within countries (ρ ≈ 0.0–0.13); the cooperative cannot move its farms either |
| Switch all production to another processing method | Differences under one point and not consistent within countries |
| Focus only on green-coffee defects | Honduras already has the lowest share of severe defects in the benchmark (3.8%) |

## Limitations

1. **Associations, not causes.** The data is observational; for example, high-altitude origins may also have better practices.
2. **Selection bias.** CQI samples are lots submitted voluntarily, usually the best ones (86.2% are specialty), so results compare groups relative to each other, not real market shares.
3. **Small Honduran sample.** 52 samples, unevenly spread over time (6–17 per year), so yearly results have wide confidence intervals.
4. **Outdated data.** Samples are from 2010–2018; varieties, practices and climate may have changed.
5. **Missing practices.** The data has no information on picking, fermentation or drying practices, so Recommendation 2's actions come from industry knowledge, not from this analysis. That is why lot-level measurement is the foundation.
6. **Self-reported fields.** Altitude was reported in 123 formats and partly converted from feet; processing method is missing for 11.6% of samples.
